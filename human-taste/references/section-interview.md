# Section interview prompts

Read this when preparing section rounds. Select only prompts that remain unresolved. Adapt examples to known facts, established direction, and participation mode; never present this entire reference as a questionnaire.

For each section: state its visitor purpose, offer concrete alternatives for the consequential unresolved decision, recommend with a reason, and record the actual answer or delegation. Include mobile behavior where it materially affects the choice. A purpose may be met by combining or removing a section.

## Header and navigation

Set brand prominence, navigation priorities, primary action, spacing, sticky behavior, and mobile navigation. Base links on real pages or destinations. Ask whether a persistent header helps a long or task-oriented page enough to justify the screen space it uses.

For a three-project architecture portfolio whose primary action is an inquiry, alternatives might be:

- **Quiet index:** a small wordmark, Work and Studio links, and a text Inquiry action; keeps photography dominant, with less persistent conversion emphasis.
- **Persistent inquiry:** a compact sticky bar with the same navigation and a clear Inquiry action; keeps contact reachable on long project stories but occupies mobile space.

Resolve mobile menu access, keyboard focus, open/close behavior, and what happens without the enhancement. Reuse the global action hierarchy.

## Hero or opening

Establish what visitors must understand immediately, the actual headline and supporting copy, primary/secondary actions, composition, imagery, and mobile crop or order. Do not assume a giant hero is needed for a catalog, documentation site, or utility.

For an independent bicycle workshop offering repairs and appointments, headline alternatives could be:

- **Direct:** "Bicycle repairs. Book a workshop appointment." Makes the service and next step explicit.
- **Conversational:** "Tell us what your bike needs." Invites a service inquiry; use only if inquiry is the actual next step.

Explain whether a text-first opening makes a practical offer clearer, or whether a real workshop image communicates something useful. Do not invent location, turnaround, pricing, or service promises to improve a headline. Mark draft copy as draft.

## Main content

Choose sections for the actual information: products, services, work, process, stories, comparisons, documentation, or another content type. Ask what visitors need to compare or understand before choosing a layout.

| Information | Possible treatment | Consequence to discuss |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Services with comparable scope | Compact rows or a comparison table | Easier comparison; long descriptions may need linked detail |
| Selected work with meaningful images | Large case-study sequence | Supports visual examination; needs authentic assets and more scrolling |
| Steps in a real process | Numbered narrative with examples | Explains sequence; avoid inventing steps to fill a template |
| Several genuinely independent items | Cards with consistent fields | Helps scanning; check whether repeated containers add useful structure |
| Long-form expertise | Readable text with captions or side notes | Supports depth; requires deliberate mobile reading order |

Ask about density, emphasis, image-to-text balance, interaction, and mobile reflow only when the direction has not settled them. Avoid ornamental dashboards that do not represent the offering or real functionality.

## Evidence and trust

First establish whether real testimonials, permitted customer logos, verifiable results, credentials, or demonstrations exist. Preserve sources and permission questions in the decision record.

If none exists, offer a factual worked example, an actual product demonstration, a transparent process explanation, or no evidence section. These must also be truthful; a sample is labeled as a sample. Never fabricate named customers, ratings, logos, quotations, percentages, or awards, even as attractive placeholders in an apparently finished page.

For an artisan without testimonials, close-up photographs of their actual materials and a factual process description may demonstrate care. Without photography, a text-led explanation can still be credible; an illustration must not masquerade as a completed commission.

## Conversion

Clarify the next action and commitment: inquiry, booking, purchase, download, signup, or another agreed outcome. Make the action text match what really happens. Explain what information is needed and what visitors should expect next using confirmed facts.

Choose between a direct contact link, an inline form, or an agreed external flow based on the task and available services. Set labels, required/optional fields, validation, loading, error recovery, and success feedback where relevant. A visual mock form cannot claim that a message was sent. Record missing endpoints, credentials, or providers before representing the interaction as complete.

On mobile, discuss field order and action placement where they affect completion; retain accessible labels and keyboard operation.

## Footer

Choose the appropriate closing message, contact information, utility/legal links, and any genuinely useful navigation. Ask which legal or utility destinations are required and supplied; flag missing ones rather than inventing policy text or obligations.

A short project page may need only contact and required links. A large resource site may warrant grouped navigation. Add a newsletter, social links, or a large sitemap only when requested or justified by the brief and backed by real destinations and services.

## Other pages and changing answers

For additional pages, inherit shared navigation, type, palette, spacing, and interaction conventions. Ask about the page's distinct purpose, content, composition, and next action, not settled global choices.

If the user changes direction, state the affected decisions: for example, replacing documentary photography with a text-led direction changes opening composition, case-study presentation, and asset requirements. Preserve unaffected facts and choices. Revise the decision record and brief; obtain revised approval if the previous brief was already approved.
